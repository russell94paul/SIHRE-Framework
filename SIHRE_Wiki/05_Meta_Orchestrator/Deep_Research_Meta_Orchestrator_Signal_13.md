## Dynamic weighting and orchestration

For your first question — learning per-strategy, per-regime, and per-instrument which signals to trust — the literature strongly supports a **context-dependent gating** approach rather than static ensemble weights. Recent work on heterogeneous mixtures of experts shows why: when experts differ in capacity or function, homogeneous routing wastes specialisation. HMoE explicitly argues that diverse input complexity benefits from experts with different capacities, while recent adaptive time-series fusion work shows that no single forecaster dominates across all samples, making sample-level adaptive fusion materially better than fixed combinations. In multimodal settings, surveys and interaction-aware MoE papers likewise show that heterogeneous signals should not simply be concatenated; they should be re-weighted according to the type of interaction they capture. citeturn23view8turn23view6turn31view0turn31view1

That is directly relevant to SIHRE because your twelve inputs are not twelve versions of the same thing. Semantic retrieval, a domain LLM, a quant model, a knowledge graph, causal inference, conformal prediction, adversarial verification, and program synthesis all fail in different ways, on different timescales, and with different computational costs. The most defensible orchestration pattern is therefore a **non-homogeneous routing stack**: first, a hard eligibility filter that turns some signals off entirely when their prerequisites are absent; then a sparse gating layer that learns soft trust weights conditional on instrument/regime/latency/coverage features; then a top-level portfolio-style combiner that turns signal forecasts into final action confidence. That design is an inference, but it is the configuration most consistent with modern MoE, adaptive fusion, and Bayesian stacking results. citeturn23view8turn18view1turn23view7turn24view0turn25view8

Contextual bandits are an especially good fit for the **selection** side of Signal 13. The seminal Yahoo! work showed that a contextual-bandit policy can adapt article selection online and can also be evaluated offline from logged randomised traffic. Later work formalised model selection in contextual bandits as its own problem, with guarantees for choosing between candidate model classes and for adapting online to the most suitable base algorithm for the current environment. In practical terms, that means Signal 13 can be interpreted as a contextual bandit whose "arms" are not just buy/sell actions, but candidate orchestration policies: different signal subsets, weighting maps, or fusion rules. citeturn18view2turn18view3turn18view4turn31view6

For **continuous weight optimisation**, Bayesian optimisation and online Bayesian stacking are the most useful complements to bandits. Bayesian optimisation has long been a strong tool for expensive weight and hyperparameter selection, and more recent work on Bayesian ensembling shows that online Bayesian stacking can adaptively combine models in a streaming setting, with an explicit link to online portfolio selection and regret analysis. Meta-Bayesian optimisation also matters for transfer: robust meta-BO can import knowledge from related tasks while down-weighting harmful dissimilar ones, which is exactly the right mathematical instinct for transferring orchestration priors across markets or strategies without assuming they are always useful. citeturn18view5turn23view7turn22view7

For a trading implementation, the best-supported design is a **three-level trust mechanism**. A regime encoder should estimate market state; a signal-quality encoder should estimate each signal's local reliability using features such as recent calibration, latency, missingness, and disagreement; and a final combiner should map those state variables into sparse trust weights. Financial adaptive-ensemble work already points in this direction: Dynamic Model Selection and Adaptive Ensemble methods explicitly take a rolling view of model selection and ensembling in changing time-series environments, rather than trying to find one globally best forecaster. citeturn24view0turn25view7turn18view6

## Drift detection and adaptation in live trading

For live trading, the literature is unusually clear on one point: **there is no single "best" drift detector**. The classical family — CUSUM, Page-Hinkley, DDM/EDDM, and ADWIN — remains foundational because each method detects a different kind of instability. ADWIN adapts its window length online and comes with explicit false-positive and false-negative guarantees; CUSUM and Page-Hinkley remain fast, sequential detectors for mean-shift style changes; and the broader survey literature still treats detectors and ensembles as the two dominant families for concept-drift handling. citeturn19view4turn19view5turn31view9turn31view8

But recent work also makes clear that drift detection is **not just a detector choice problem**. Large 2025–26 surveys argue that adaptive ensembles and meta-learning strategies outperform static architectures under combined drift and forgetting, while the "Window Dilemma" paper shows why many drift events are partly artefacts of the chosen windowing scheme. In other words, for a live trading system, a drift alarm should not be treated as ontological truth; it should be treated as evidence that the current adaptation policy may no longer be adequate. That is an important distinction for Signal 13 because it suggests the orchestrator should reason over drift evidence, not merely react to a binary alarm. citeturn18view7turn25view3turn18view8turn25view4

In finance specifically, the strongest open-literature direction is **regime-aware online change detection**, not generic stream mining. Work on online market regime detection frames the problem as recognising in real time when the data-generating process has shifted to a different market regime. Financial quickest change-point detection papers show that classic sequential surveillance tools work on real-world market data, and structural-break research extends CUSUM-style logic into high-dimensional, cross-sectionally correlated time series. Put simply: state of the art in finance is moving away from a single scalar alarm and towards a layered view that includes market-regime change, structural break, and forecast-performance deterioration at the same time. citeturn25view5turn25view6turn19view3

The most convincing recent adaptation papers also treat drift as **heterogeneous**. Proceed shows that in online forecasting, delayed labels create a temporal gap that itself induces concept drift; DynaME distinguishes recurring drift from emergent drift and handles them with different expert mechanisms; DriftMoE uses an online MoE to adapt under concept drift on the fly. For a trading system, this distinction matters a great deal: a recurring volatility regime should reactivate archived signal combinations, while a truly emergent market structure should down-weight all specialist memories and fall back to a more conservative generalist. citeturn18view9turn30view6turn19view0

That leads to a practical answer to your retraining-trigger question. The best current approach is **statistically grounded multi-channel monitoring**: monitor feature drift, performance drift, uncertainty drift, and economic-value drift separately. Recent post-deployment monitoring work argues that degradation detection should be framed explicitly as hypothesis testing, while "learning debt" work proposes a cost-sensitive retraining decision based on divergence between a continuously updated shadow posterior and the frozen production posterior. There is also active work on unsupervised expected-utility drift detectors that decide whether retraining is warranted even without post-deployment labels. citeturn32view4turn32view5turn30view5

For Signal 13, the most defensible live-trading design is therefore a **four-stage drift loop**. First, light sequential detectors such as CUSUM/Page-Hinkley/ADWIN watch fast statistics. Secondly, regime and structure monitors assess whether the market itself has changed. Thirdly, uncertainty monitors check whether conformal coverage or forecast interval efficiency is deteriorating; recent online conformal work, including OLCP-Hedge, is especially relevant here because it adapts online under heterogeneity and treats bandwidth choice as an expert-aggregation problem. Fourthly, only after those signals are combined should the orchestrator decide between re-weighting, warm-start retraining, or human escalation. citeturn19view5turn25view5turn25view9turn32view4turn32view5

## Search, experimentation, and pruning

On ensemble composition, the answer is yes: **Signal 13 can learn that combinations matter, not just individual signals**. Differentiable NAS, evolutionary search, and reinforcement-learning-based architecture search all exist for discovering useful structures that humans would not specify upfront. DARTS remains the canonical example of making architecture search efficient via continuous relaxation, and the broader HPO/NAS literature now treats search itself as a first-class optimisation problem with model-, bandit-, population-, and gradient-based methods. citeturn2search3turn24view9

In trading, the public literature is early but real. RegimeNAS applies differentiable architecture search to cryptocurrency trading and explicitly integrates regime awareness, Bayesian search, and real-time regime-specific block selection. EvoPort combines randomised alpha discovery, ensemble model search, and stochastic allocation in one evolutionary pipeline, while AutoAlpha uses hierarchical evolutionary search to mine formulaic alphas and then feeds them into an ensemble ranking model. Those systems are not yet the same as your Meta-Orchestrator, but they are direct precedents for the idea that a trading stack can search over strategy components and signal compositions rather than treating the model graph as fixed. citeturn23view3turn23view4turn23view5

The more important question is not whether search is possible, but whether it is **safe from meta-overfitting**. Here the literature offers a warning. NAS papers have explicitly documented overfitting in architecture search, accuracy predictors can themselves overfit, and recent HPO work formalises "overtuning" as overfitting at the optimisation layer rather than the base-model layer. In the 2025 overtuning study, roughly one in ten runs selected a configuration that generalised worse than the default or first configuration tried. For Signal 13, that means architecture search must run under stronger statistical discipline than plain model training, because the orchestrator is optimising over a much richer search space than any one signal. citeturn32view0turn32view1turn32view2

That is where **continuous automated A/B testing** becomes essential. The state of the art in online experimentation is not "run a fixed A/B test and peek occasionally"; it is continuous monitoring with always-valid inference, newer sequential tests such as YEAST, and staged rollouts through shadow, canary, and traffic-sliced deployment. Always-valid inference has already been implemented at large scale, and modern practice in ML operations consistently places shadow mode before low-percentage live exposure. In a quant context, that naturally maps to paper trading, shadow trading against live market data, and then very small capital ramps. citeturn15search1turn20view0turn24view8turn20view3turn24view7

The right promotion logic for Signal 13 is therefore not "winner by Sharpe". It should be a **gated experiment ladder**: offline replay and off-policy evaluation on historical logs; then paper/shadow trading where the candidate architecture emits full decisions but executes nothing; then low-notional canary capital; then progressive ramp-up. Doubly robust off-policy evaluation is the standard way to get less biased use from logs, and software rollout research shows that staged release itself can be automated with reinforcement learning under multi-objective constraints. In practice, the orchestration candidates should only compete on live capital once they clear shadow and sequential-testing criteria. citeturn20view1turn24view6turn20view2

On **self-pruning**, the literature is again supportive. Modern MoE work explicitly notes that not all experts are equal and that expert-level pruning and skipping can reduce cost while preserving performance. Broader pruning surveys distinguish between static and dynamic pruning and between criterion-based and learned sparsification. For Signal 13, the correct analogue is not pruning neurons but pruning signals: if a signal's marginal contribution, net of latency and compute, is effectively zero in the current market/environment, the orchestrator should be able to set its effective weight to zero and stop paying for it. citeturn23view9turn20view7

The key caveat is that pruning in a drifting market should usually be **reversible**. A signal that is useless during a low-dispersion grind may become critical during an event-driven dislocation. So the right mechanism is not permanent deletion; it is "soft retirement" with archived priors, activation histories, and fast re-entry hooks. That recommendation is a synthesis of the pruning, continual-learning, and drift literatures rather than a single published recipe, but it is the safest design. citeturn23view9turn21view8turn18view7

## Transfer, update cadence, and recursive improvement

On transfer learning, the available evidence is encouraging. QuantNet explicitly aims to learn market-agnostic trends that improve market-specific trading strategies, while more recent work on zero-shot financial time-series forecasting uses meta-learning to learn initialisations that adapt rapidly to new tasks with minimal data. A new online portfolio-selection paper goes further and directly meta-learns initial parameters and mixture weights for multiple portfolio policies, claiming good transferability across time periods and markets. The large systematic review on transfer learning in financial prediction reaches the broader conclusion that transfer methods can improve performance if source-domain choice is done carefully. citeturn20view9turn25view0turn21view1turn25view1

For Signal 13, that means the answer to "does learning optimal weights for Strategy A help Strategy B?" is **yes, but mainly as an initial prior, not as a universal final policy**. Meta-learned initial weights can dramatically reduce cold-start pain for new instruments or strategies, especially when the orchestrator is transferring more abstract objects such as regime embeddings, uncertainty calibration behaviour, or patterns of signal disagreement, rather than raw trade rules. The robust way to do that is to transfer only through a similarity model that can also identify harmful source tasks, which is exactly the intuition behind robust meta-BO. citeturn21view1turn25view0turn22view7

Across traders, desks, or counterparties, federated methods become relevant. Finance-specific federated forecasting papers already show that volatile market-trend prediction can be done collaboratively without sharing raw data, and the federated transfer-learning literature clarifies the broader space for private cross-party knowledge transfer. When streaming and forgetting are added, federated continual learning becomes the appropriate frame: the core challenge becomes spatial-temporal catastrophic forgetting, meaning the system must fuse knowledge across both different clients and different time periods. That is extremely relevant if Signal 13 is meant to pool meta-knowledge across multiple strategies or traders while respecting data silos. citeturn24view3turn25view2turn30view7turn30view8

On **online learning versus batch retraining**, the most important fact is often ignored: true online learning is only feasible when labels arrive quickly enough to support reliable recursion. Modern stream-learning surveys explicitly note that online learning is not possible when labels are missing or delayed, and delayed-feedback bandit papers formalise the resulting regret penalties. In trading, the delay is structural: the "reward" of a decision is not known until the holding period, transaction costs, and execution slippage have all played out. That makes fully online reweighting possible only for short-horizon or proxy-labelled objectives. citeturn30view2turn30view1turn32view6

So the right answer for Signal 13 is a **hybrid cadence**. Update lightweight trust weights online from immediate proxies — forecast residuals, fill quality, disagreement, uncertainty coverage, regime similarity, latency, and capital usage. Update heavier meta-learner parameters in micro-batches as delayed trade outcomes arrive. Rebuild the deeper architecture or routing logic in larger governed batches. Papers on online forecasting have been quite explicit that naïve online updates can leak future information, damage performance, or adapt to stale concepts when labels are delayed, so the orchestration layer should separate "fast control" from "slow learning". citeturn18view9turn30view9turn27search1

On **recursive self-improvement**, the honest answer is "partly, but within tight bounds". It is plausible for Signal 13 to improve its own meta-learning algorithm via governed search over routing rules, optimiser choices, calibration schemes, memory policies, and experiment criteria. Recent self-evolving agent work, including Gödel Agent and Darwin Gödel Machine, makes that possibility explicit. But the strongest theoretical result I found cuts against open-ended optimism: formal work on self-improving LLMs shows that if exogenous grounded signal vanishes, closed-loop self-improvement tends towards degenerative dynamics through entropy decay and variance amplification. In other words, bounded systems can self-improve, but they do not escape the need for external reality checks. citeturn21view5turn23view2turn21view4

For a trading platform, that means recursive improvement should be limited to a **governed AutoML envelope**. Signal 13 may search over meta-algorithms, but only inside a fixed search space, under fixed risk constraints, under shadow-mode validation, and with rollback. Recent work on layered mutability in self-modifying agents and on governance for staged capability evolution supports exactly that posture. The theoretical limit is therefore not only compute; it is also boundedness of search space, bounded feedback quality, bounded capital risk, and bounded tolerance for identity drift in the production system. citeturn21view9turn15search3turn15search14turn24view9

## Precedents, risks, and a recommended blueprint

There is no openly documented system that fully matches your definition of a **self-improving heterogeneous ensemble for quant trading**. The closest in-domain public precedents are partial: adaptive model-selection and ensembling methods for financial time series, transfer across systematic trading strategies, mixture-policy portfolio selection with meta-learning, and evolutionary alpha/model search in portfolio pipelines. All of those attack slices of the problem, but none of them evidently orchestrates twelve heterogeneous reasoning modalities with closed-loop drift handling, self-pruning, shadow experimentation, and governed promotion. That gap is precisely what your uploaded documents identify as the novelty target. citeturn24view0turn24view4turn21view1turn23view4turn23view5 fileciteturn0file5

Outside finance, however, there are strong analogues. In recommendation systems, contextual bandits are a mature mechanism for continuously adapting decisions from feedback, and recent industrial papers report production A/B wins and even self-evolving recommendation frameworks deployed at scale. In autonomous driving, MoE systems such as GEMINUS and CBDES use routers to select specialised experts by scenario. In drug discovery, systems such as BATCHIE and recent closed-loop molecular-generation work use active learning and Bayesian updates to decide what evidence to collect next. In robotics and embodied AI, "self-evolving embodied AI" is now a named paradigm. If I had to name the **closest existing system class**, I would split the answer. The closest *production analogue* is probably the recommendation-system stack: contextual-bandit control, offline evaluation from logged data, shadow/A/B deployment, and incremental promotion are all much more mature there than in public quant research. The closest *domain analogue* in finance is the combination of adaptive financial ensembles, transfer/meta-learning over strategies, and evolutionary alpha/architecture search. The risks are substantial. The first is **overfitting to recent regimes**: drift handling that is too recency-biased can destroy robustness to recurring states, which newer drift papers explicitly warn about. The second is **meta-overfitting**: the orchestrator can over-optimise its own validation and search process, as NAS/HPO overfitting work now documents. The third is **catastrophic forgetting**: signals that were once useful can be driven to zero and never recover, even though their edge was merely dormant. The architecture I would recommend for Signal 13 is therefore intentionally conservative. The orchestrator should maintain a **state vector** containing regime embeddings, per-signal calibration/error history, compute/latency costs, market microstructure conditions, and signal disagreement. It should output four things only: trust weights, abstention decisions, retraining/search recommendations, and promotion decisions. It should not directly rewrite base-signal code in production. Fast paths should be online and low-capacity; heavy search and architecture changes should live in paper/shadow environments; and every promoted change should clear off-policy evaluation, sequential significance, and bounded-risk capital ramps. ## Open questions and limits

The main limitation of the current evidence base is that many of the most directly relevant systems — especially in 2025–26 trading, self-evolving agents, and online conformal adaptation — are still preprints rather than mature benchmark standards. The finance-specific literature on Meta-Orchestrator-style systems is therefore promising but thin, and strong claims of production superiority should be treated as provisional. citeturn19view6turn23view4turn23view5turn23view0turn25view9

A second limitation is that the literature remains fragmented. We have good papers for routing, drift, transfer, search, pruning, federated continual learning, staged rollout, and self-evolving agents — but not yet a single public paper showing these capabilities integrated in a live, risk-governed trading ensemble. That means any implementation of Signal 13 will still require original systems engineering and unusually careful evaluation design. citeturn18view7turn30view7turn15search14turn24view7turn22view4
