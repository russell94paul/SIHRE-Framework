# Novel Quant Trading Technology Beyond Conventional Backtesting

## The central finding

Your SIHRE concept is directionally ahead of the market. The pieces already exist in isolation: automated strategy generation and filtering in StrategyQuant and Build Alpha; hypothesis-led research workflows in QuantConnect; experiment lineage and promotion tooling in MLflow; and increasingly agentic, evolutionary search systems such as QuantEvolve and ProFiT. What I did **not** find is a mainstream systematic-trading platform that closes the whole loop from **post-trade loss forensics → structured hypothesis generation → automatic strategy mutation → governed backtest promotion into a registry**. ## Edge forensics and closed-loop hypothesis generation

The closest commercial analogue to your "Edge Forensics" idea is not a full hypothesis engine, but a collection of adjacent features. Build Alpha exposes a **Signal Breakdown** that shows which signals occur most often during winning or losing trades; it also has an **Intelligence Report** that reviews saved strategies and suggests modifications. StrategyQuant formalises a workflow of generating strategies, evaluating them, then retesting or optimising survivors, with cross-checks applied during generation for automatic filtering. These are meaningful steps towards research automation, but they still stop short of explicitly phrasing a hypothesis such as "London-session entries under low displacement underperform" and auto-compiling that into a new testable rule variant. citeturn27view0turn22view3turn22view0

QuantConnect's own research guidance remains explicitly **human hypothesis-first**: define a causal thesis, then test it in research notebooks before backtesting. That is important because it shows the mainstream research stack still assumes humans originate the thesis. Your proposed system differs by making the **trade ledger itself** the source of hypothesis discovery. I did not find a documented trading platform where the losing trade population is automatically mined into structured causal clauses and then promoted into a governed experiment queue. citeturn26search0turn26search3

The best way to think about Edge Forensics, based on the current building blocks, is as a synthesis of three existing worlds. First, you need an experiment backbone like MLflow, which already logs runs, parameters, metrics, artefacts, lineage, versioning and promotion metadata. Secondly, you need a reasoning layer closer to FinCARE, which combines statistical causal discovery, a financial knowledge graph and LLM reasoning for hypothesis generation. Thirdly, you need a domain knowledge layer closer to FinReflectKG, which turns filings and structured finance documents into a reusable knowledge graph. The genuinely novel move is to apply that stack **not** to company-level analytics, but to **strategy failure modes and execution cohorts**. If you build this, the scientifically strongest version is not "LLM reads losing trades and suggests tweaks". ## Personalised Monte Carlo and risk twinning

The state of practice has already moved beyond naïve "shuffle daily returns and inspect drawdowns". StrategyQuant separates **trade-list manipulation Monte Carlo** from **retest Monte Carlo**. The former shuffles the realised trade list or skips trades to estimate sequence dependence and missed-trade risk; the latter re-runs the strategy under randomised spreads, slippage, parameters and perturbed history. Build Alpha similarly offers Monte Carlo tests, variance testing, noise tests, equity projection bands, synthetic data and randomised out-of-sample analysis. These are all materially closer to your idea of strategy-specific, realised-trade simulation than textbook portfolio-theory Monte Carlo. citeturn22view1turn22view2turn22view3

One particularly relevant practitioner innovation is StrategyQuant's 2025 **MACHR Block Randomisation**, which explicitly randomises **blocks of consecutive trades** rather than individual trades, preserving internal regime structure while changing the sequence of regimes. It is designed to answer exactly the question your specification raises: "what if the same market conditions occurred, but in a different order?" That is not yet a full personalised risk engine, but it is a concrete example of regime-conditional resampling in the direction you want. citeturn28view0

In academic risk modelling, the nearest equivalents are regime-switching and conditional simulation methods. The MSGARCH framework supports fitting and simulating Markov-switching GARCH models and has been shown to improve VaR and Expected Shortfall forecasting relative to single-regime models; earlier work on high-frequency market regimes also found distinct trade-return distributions conditional on market state. There are also Bayesian trading/risk procedures that explicitly propagate model and estimation uncertainty into return/risk assessment, rather than treating the fitted model as fixed truth. citeturn5search1turn5search9turn5search16turn5search7

The newest strand is synthetic scenario generation: conditional GANs, TimeVAE-style models, diffusion models for financial time series, and finance-specific time-series foundation-model work that uses synthetic augmentation. Here the evidence is promising but still immature. The practical lesson is that "personalised Monte Carlo" should probably be a **hybrid**: empirical trade bootstraps for faithfulness, regime/block resampling for path dependence, and generative paths for tail and novelty stress tests. That synthesis is more robust than relying on any one simulator family alone. citeturn5search10turn5search2turn13search3turn21search19

So, in direct response to your question, the state of the art is **not** a universally adopted personalised Monte Carlo stack for quant platforms. It is an emerging toolkit of trade-aware Monte Carlo, synthetic data, regime-switching risk models and conditional generative scenarios. Your opportunity is to turn those into a **risk twin** specific to the individual strategy, contract, session, account size, execution venue and holding-period regime. citeturn22view1turn22view2turn28view0turn5search1turn5search10

## Honest validation and overfitting control

Deflated Sharpe Ratio remains one of the most important practical tools in this space because it explicitly corrects for two inflation sources that matter enormously in systematic research: non-normal returns and selection bias under multiple testing. Bailey and López de Prado's CSCV framework, presented in *What to Look for in a Backtest*, complements this with Probability of Backtest Overfitting style diagnostics. Older but still foundational safeguards include White's Reality Check, Hansen's SPA test, and stepwise SPA variants designed to control data-snooping bias when comparing many strategy candidates. Harvey and Liu add a further layer by proposing Sharpe "haircuts" and profit hurdles that account for the fact that a reported backtest is almost never the first strategy tested. citeturn1search12turn1search10turn2search0turn2search16turn32view3

Recent work strengthens the case for logging the **entire search process**, not just the winning strategy. Harvey, Liu and Zhu's broader false-discovery literature shows that data-specific cutoffs and double-bootstrap evaluation can better assess Type I and Type II errors in financial multiple testing. At the same time, López de Prado's 2026 false-discovery work argues that the FDR cannot be identified from in-sample statistics alone when results arise from a search-and-selection process. That is highly relevant to your platform: the registry of all attempted ideas is not operational bureaucracy; it is statistically necessary metadata. citeturn32view2turn2search3turn2search14

On CSCV and CPCV specifically, the most balanced reading is this: they are powerful, but they are **not** a magic stamp of "now the backtest is honest". A 2024 synthetic comparison found Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation superior to K-fold, Purged K-fold and especially plain walk-forward in reducing overfitting risk and improving DSR/PBO diagnostics in that controlled setting. Useful evidence, yes. Final truth, no. Markets are non-stationary, labels overlap, and one validation geometry can still miss a regime break that another catches. citeturn20search0turn20search2

A genuinely modern validation stack therefore looks layered rather than singular: immutable experiment registry; purging/embargoing where labels overlap; DSR and PBO-style diagnostics; White/SPA family tests for broad search spaces; strict cost/funding/slippage sensitivity; synthetic/regime perturbation tests; and finally live-paper shadowing before promotion. That is an inference from the literature, but it is well supported by the way these methods solve different failure modes rather than the same one. citeturn1search12turn1search10turn2search0turn20search0turn29view5

One additional frontier worth watching is **e-backtesting**. In 2025, Wang, Wang and Ziegel introduced model-free backtesting based on e-values and e-processes for risk measure forecasts; follow-on work extends this to standard and comparative e-backtests over a wide class of risk measures. This line is aimed more at risk-forecast validation than alpha discovery, but conceptually it points towards a broader idea: sequential evidence accumulation rather than one-shot significance testing. That is attractive for live monitoring of deployed strategies as well as research-phase validation. citeturn31search0turn31search8turn31academia17

## Temporal integrity and anti-lookahead architecture

Making the strategy function signature incapable of reading future bars is an excellent start, but it only eliminates one class of leakage. A large fraction of real-world lookahead comes from **data plumbing**, not signal code: point-in-time joins, stale fundamental updates, retroactive revisions, contract metadata, corporate actions, and feature materialisation. Modern ML/feature systems now treat this explicitly. Feast's point-in-time joins reproduce feature state "as of" a historical timestamp by scanning backward; Databricks' time-series feature tables use AS-OF logic and warn that future values leaking into training rows silently impair model validity; XTDB's bitemporality separates valid-time from transaction-time so you can ask not only "what was true then?" but also "what did the system know then?"; and event sourcing gives an append-only audit trail from which historical state can be reconstructed exactly. citeturn24view0turn24view1turn24view2turn24view3

Architecturally, the most robust pattern is therefore a **temporal stack**: append-only event log; bitemporal reference store; point-in-time feature retrieval; deterministic chronological replay; and execution semantics that force orders to be placed only after the data used to decide them. Event-driven backtesting engines already embody part of this. Zipline explicitly positions itself as a stream-based system that processes events individually to avoid look-ahead bias, and its examples flow data chronologically through the algorithm. QuantConnect also serialises data and order handling through event handlers, while warning that live and backtest timing differ in important ways for scheduled events and fills. AutoQuant pushes the idea further by hard-coding strict T+1 execution semantics and no-look-ahead funding alignment. citeturn15search5turn25view2turn24view7turn25view1turn29view5

A second class of guardrails is **detection tooling**. Freqtrade's lookahead-analysis exists precisely because dataframe-style backtesting can compute indicators over the full series and silently smuggle future information into present decisions; its recursive-analysis adds further checks on indicator recursion and a limited lookahead check. In the LLM space, Look-Ahead-Bench shows the same issue in a new form, demonstrating that standard foundation models can enjoy substantial temporal contamination in financial workflows and that point-in-time evaluation is necessary. citeturn24view4turn24view5turn25view0

The most promising anti-lookahead pattern beyond your function signature is a **typed strategy DSL with temporal semantics baked in**. Continuous Program Search's GPTL is a useful example: grammatically closed, statically typed, deterministic, evaluation at next-bar open, and invalid by construction if it steps outside the language. That kind of constrained language can make entire classes of leakage impossible rather than merely discouraged. citeturn30view4turn30view5

## Automated strategy synthesis beyond classic GP

The literature has now moved well beyond vanilla genetic programming. In 2024, **Alpha²** framed formulaic alpha discovery as program construction guided by deep reinforcement learning and explicitly addressed validity and alpha-correlation concerns that GP often handles poorly. In early 2026, **Alpha Discovery via Grammar-Guided Learning and Search** introduced what it calls AlphaCFG: a context-free grammar with semantic constraints, plus grammar-aware Monte Carlo Tree Search guided by syntax-sensitive value and policy networks. That is materially more sophisticated than "mutate random indicator trees and hope". citeturn29view1turn30view2turn30view3

The next layer is **neuro-symbolic and agentic search**. FAMA uses cross-sample selection and chain-of-experience mechanisms to make LLM-based factor mining less homogeneous and more cumulative; AlphaPROBE reframes factor mining as navigation over a DAG of ancestral factor relations rather than a flat pool; QuantEvolve combines multi-agent hypothesis generation with quality-diversity optimisation so the platform searches for **many** strong strategies aligned to different investor preferences rather than a single top fitness score; and ProFiT uses LLM-driven code mutation, self-analysis and walk-forward validation in a closed loop over liquid futures. citeturn29view7turn29view2turn29view3turn29view4

There is also a newer line that sits between symbolic search and continuous optimisation. Continuous Program Search introduces a compact DSL, learns a block-factorised latent embedding of strategies, and then performs structured mutations in continuous space while keeping programs valid by construction. That matters because it replaces brittle syntax-level mutation with geometry-aware search over semantically meaningful subspaces. FactorMiner pushes in a similar direction by giving an agent reusable compositional skills plus experience memory, so search compounds over time instead of repeatedly rediscovering the same patterns. citeturn30view4turn30view5turn16search4

For your use case, the key architectural implication is that **event primitives should probably be first-class grammar symbols**, not free-form code fragments. FVGs, sweeps, order blocks, displacement states, catalyst windows, and session tags can be modelled as typed building blocks with explicit temporal meaning. Then the search engine can operate over valid compositions only. That is much closer to AlphaCFG, GPTL and AlphaPROBE than to old-school GP, and it is almost certainly the right design if you want automated strategy generation without exploding the space into unverifiable spaghetti. citeturn30view2turn30view3turn30view4turn29view2

The main caveat is that much of the newest work still targets **cross-sectional formulaic alpha mining**, not deployable intraday event strategies with fills, queueing, roll logic, slippage and contract nuances. ProFiT and AutoQuant help bridge that gap, but the literature is still young. So the real research frontier is not generic GP replacement; it is **execution-aware, typed, event-native synthesis under temporal and microstructure constraints**. citeturn29view4turn29view5turn30view4

## Catalysts, fundamentals, and alternative data as first-class features

A modern quant platform should treat fundamental event data as a **temporal entity system**, not as side-channel notes attached to price bars. The raw feature space already exists in vendor and exchange infrastructure: the Federal Reserve publishes the scheduled FOMC calendar and release cadence; Trading Economics' calendar exposes actual, previous and consensus values from official sources; earnings datasets now include actuals, estimates, surprise metrics and timestamps; CME's calendars expose expiration, first/last trade and delivery dates; and corporate fundamentals are available point-in-time within engines such as QuantConnect. citeturn27view6turn27view2turn27view3turn27view4turn27view5turn27view7

The feature engineering that emerges from the literature is richer than "actual minus expected". Scotti's work constructs real-time surprise and uncertainty indexes from macro releases. Kroner shows that **investor attention** changes how macro news is priced. Menkveld and co-authors show that announcement surprise and customer order flow matter materially in Treasury futures, while ECB evidence documents pre-announcement drift and informed trading before some major releases. BIS work on FOMC announcements finds volume falls ahead of the release and rises afterwards, consistent with changing information asymmetry and liquidity behaviour. In equities, Sheng shows macro news and firm-specific earnings news are complementary rather than separate; and Brandt et al. show that the stock-return reaction around earnings, EAR, contains information beyond classic SUE, suggesting market response itself can be a better feature than the accounting number alone. citeturn33view0turn33view2turn33view4turn33view5turn33view3turn33view1turn33view7

That implies a practical feature hierarchy. At the **event-clock** level: time to next event, time since last event, event family, session overlap, market-open/closed status, expiry or roll window status. At the **surprise** level: signed surprise, absolute surprise, standardised surprise relative to forecast dispersion or release history, revision surprise, and whether the sign is risk-on or risk-off for the asset. At the **market-state** level: prevailing trend, realised volatility, options positioning, liquidity, breadth, order flow and attention into the event. At the **impact-prior** level: learned historical response curves for each event family by asset, time-of-day and surprise bucket. None of that is exotic; it is simply the natural encoding implied by the empirical event literature above. citeturn27view2turn33view0turn33view2turn33view4turn33view3

On your specific question about combining structural market events with fundamental catalysts, the literature strongly supports the **catalyst side** but not directly your preferred structural primitives. I did not find peer-reviewed work directly studying ICT-style FVGs, sweeps or order blocks conditioned on earnings surprise or FOMC decisions. What the literature does show is that scheduled macro and micro news reshape jumps, volume, order flow, liquidity and post-announcement drift. The closest synthesis is therefore a design principle rather than an established paper: treat structural patterns as the **execution geometry** and catalysts as the **state/gating variables** that decide when those geometries are worth trading. citeturn33view4turn33view5turn33view6turn33view1turn27view1

Alternative data is relevant here, but selectively. ## What a next-generation quant platform should actually be

If this platform were built today from first principles, I would not describe it as a "backtester with AI features". I would describe it as a **temporal research operating system**. Its core would be a bitemporal event store plus point-in-time retrieval layer; above that, a knowledge graph connecting symbols, contracts, macro events, company events, strategies, primitives, experiments and live incidents; above that, a causal and generative reasoning layer using knowledge-graph constraints, causal discovery and LLM assistance; and above that, a typed strategy language supporting safe synthesis, mutation and audit. The ingredients exist separately in XTDB-style bitemporality, Feast/Databricks point-in-time feature retrieval, MLflow registry/governance, FinReflectKG, FinCARE, and the new search papers. What does **not** exist yet is a coherent financial platform that unifies them. citeturn24view0turn24view1turn24view2turn22view4turn22view5turn23view0turn23view1

The model stack should also be opinionated. General-purpose TSFMs and LLMs are not enough. The latest finance TSFM evidence suggests that off-the-shelf pre-trained time-series foundation models perform poorly in zero-shot and fine-tuning settings on financial markets, while models pre-trained specifically on financial data do materially better; synthetic augmentation also helps. Likewise, work on Look-Ahead-Bench implies that financial LLM evaluation must be point-in-time and contamination-aware. So the platform should use **finance-specific pretraining, point-in-time model snapshots, and temporal red-team benchmarking** as default practice, not optional hygiene. citeturn21search19turn21search4turn25view0

For your SIHRE architecture specifically, the missing product category is this: a platform that treats research as a governed closed loop in which every trade outcome updates a temporal knowledge graph; every losing cohort is eligible for automatic subgroup and causal analysis; every accepted hypothesis is translated into a typed strategy delta; every delta is evaluated under CPCV/walk-forward, DSR/PBO, cost sensitivity, regime-aware Monte Carlo and temporal leakage checks; and only then is the winner promoted as a champion or challenger with full lineage. Build Alpha, StrategyQuant, QuantConnect, MLflow, AutoQuant, ProFiT and the newer alpha-mining papers each implement one slice of that picture. None appears to implement the whole picture.
